Before Buying a Serious Greenhouse

A larger greenhouse purchase deserves a written plan, clear specifications, and answers you can compare. Learn how to check a quote, ask useful supplier questions, prepare a site, and avoid discovering missing parts of the project after delivery.

3125 words · 16 min read · Read, try, reflect

The useful idea first

A larger greenhouse purchase deserves a written plan, clear specifications, and answers you can compare. Learn how to check a quote, ask useful supplier questions, prepare a site, and avoid discovering missing parts of the project after delivery.

What you will be able to do

  • Turn a growing goal into a written list of essential greenhouse requirements.
  • Compare complete quotes, installation duties, climate controls, and service support.
  • Create a delivery, setup, safety, and first-season acceptance plan before placing an order.

Words worth knowing

Specification
A written description of the size, material, performance, or work a product or project must provide.
Scope of work
A list of the jobs and materials included in an agreement.
Load
A force a structure must handle, such as wind or snow.
Exclusion
Something a quote does not include.
Commissioning
Checking that installed equipment and systems work as intended before regular use.
Warranty
A written promise with conditions about repair or replacement. Its coverage must be read, not assumed.

Write a project brief before requesting quotes

A serious greenhouse purchase begins with a short description of what the building must do. Keep the description practical. Name the crops, the growing season, the amount of space, and the people who will use it. Include any access needs and the time available for routine care. This becomes the project brief.

Separate essential needs from appealing extras. An essential might be a door wide enough for your equipment, a safe water supply, or a ventilation arrangement you can operate. An extra might be a particular trim color or a bench style you could add later. The difference helps you protect the main purpose when choices become expensive.

Give the same brief to every supplier. If each company is solving a different problem, their quotes will be hard to compare. Ask them to point out where their proposal meets the brief and where it does not. Clear written differences are more useful than a general promise that a package should work.

Set the crop and climate requirements

Plants need a range of conditions, and their needs can change with growth stage. List the crops you intend to start with, then find suitable local guidance for temperature, light, water, and spacing. Do not choose the building only around a temperature that feels pleasant to a person standing inside.

Mark the months when each crop will occupy the space. Winter use, early seedling production, and summer growing can require different controls. A structure that supports one job may need extra equipment for another. University of New Hampshire Extension points out that even small sunny structures can need substantial attention to heat and ventilation.

Give the supplier those use periods and ask for the assumptions behind the proposed controls. What outdoor conditions were considered? What happens on a clear cold night or a bright warm day? If a result depends on added heat, shade, fans, or other equipment, make those items visible in the proposal.

Check the site before fixing the footprint

Map the space with the actual building dimensions, not only the planted area you hope to gain. Include doors, service access, gutters, equipment, and the room needed for installation. Mark property boundaries, overhead hazards, buried services, and nearby trees or structures. Some of these details may require professional location or review.

Visit during rain and at several times of day. Notice drainage and seasonal shade. A sunny site with poor access or a low, wet corner may create problems that are costly to correct. Ask what site work is required before the structure can be installed, and who is qualified to judge that work.

Before ordering, check local requirements with the appropriate building or planning office. Rules can depend on size, location, foundation, utilities, and intended use. This article cannot give a permit answer for every property. Get the answer for your site, keep the written guidance, and include any required review in the schedule.

Read the frame as a complete system

The frame, connections, foundation, and covering work together. A strong-looking piece of metal does not explain how the whole structure handles weather. Ask for the system's written specifications and the conditions under which those specifications apply. Do not treat advertising words as an engineering statement.

Ask how the greenhouse connects to the ground or foundation. Find out which anchors, bracing, and fasteners are required. Ask whether those parts are included and whether the design must change for your local conditions. Any required foundation or engineering work belongs in the project before delivery.

Look closely at moving and joining parts in a real installed example if possible. Doors, vents, corners, and panel edges reveal practical details you may miss in a distant photograph. Ask what needs adjustment after installation and how often the maker recommends inspection. A good quote should support a workable structure, not simply a pile of materials.

Compare coverings by maintenance as well as appearance

Glass, rigid plastic panels, and flexible film each have different handling and care needs. Ask about the exact covering supplied, how it attaches, and how a damaged section is replaced. Compare the entire covering system, including seals, fasteners, and edge details, rather than one material name.

Find out how the maker expects you to clean it. Some materials or surface treatments may be harmed by an unsuitable cleaner, brush, or chemical. Ask whether a local installer can replace parts or whether replacement must come from one supplier. Keep the product's care instructions with the building records.

Also ask how the covering affects the crop environment and the operating plan. Light and heat-loss claims need context. A covering choice that seems attractive in the package price may change heating needs, replacement work, or the amount of regular care. Use written information and ask for help interpreting claims you cannot compare fairly.

Give ventilation its own drawing

Ask for a sketch that shows where air enters, where warm air leaves, and what operates each opening or fan. Ventilation is a system, not just a count of vents. Crop height, bench placement, screens, and door position can affect how useful the openings are in daily practice.

Discuss the times when nobody is home. Manual equipment needs a person. Automatic equipment needs proper setup, power or another working mechanism, regular checks, and a way to handle failure. Ask what happens if a vent cannot open, a fan stops, or a control reading is wrong.

Do not wait until the greenhouse is full to discover that a handle is out of reach. Include access for checking, cleaning, and repair on the drawing. Ask the installer to demonstrate the full operating range after setup. You should understand both routine use and the safe steps to take when the system is not behaving as expected.

Keep heating and electrical work explicit

If the project includes heat, write the plant goal and the assumed outdoor conditions into the design discussion. Heating needs depend on the structure, covering, use, and weather. A small heater shown in a photograph is not a complete heating plan for your project.

Ask qualified professionals about equipment choice, fuel or power supply, wiring, controls, and safety. Greenhouses contain water, moist surfaces, growing materials, and people carrying tools. Electrical and fuel work should follow the requirements that apply to your location and equipment. Do not plan the project around improvised extension cords or unapproved heat sources.

Make the responsibility clear. Is the supplier providing the heater, installing it, or only suggesting it? Who checks the controls? What work must another contractor do first? Include service access and safe shutdown instructions. These details belong in the written scope rather than a promise to sort them out after assembly.

Plan water from source to drain

Write down where water comes from and how it reaches the crop. Include shutoffs, filtration if needed, lines, hoses, and any seasonal protection. If the project depends on water that is not already available at the site, resolve that work before calling the greenhouse ready to use.

Think about where extra water goes. Runoff from benches, cleaning water, and roof drainage all need attention. Keep paths firm and easy to clean. A floor plan should support safe walking and should not send water toward a building foundation or a neighboring property.

Ask how irrigation will be checked and adjusted. A timer is a control, not a promise that every plant receives the correct amount. Different crops and containers may need separate treatment. Leave room to inspect connections and see the water pattern. If growing in soil, include soil testing and drainage in the water discussion.

Draw access at real human size

A door measurement tells only part of the access story. Measure the approach, threshold, turn, and path beyond the door. Carry the tools or trays you expect to use through a temporary outline of the space. This simple test often reveals a tight corner before it becomes permanent.

Include the needs of everyone who will use the greenhouse. Someone may need room for a mobility aid, a place to sit, a lower work surface, or a shorter reach. Accessible design is useful for many gardeners, including people carrying heavy pots or working while tired.

Place controls where they can be reached safely. Avoid requiring a person to climb over plants to operate a vent or shut off water. Leave service space around equipment. A layout that is attractive when empty can become awkward at full crop size, so draw mature plant heights and the largest containers, not just small starter plants.

Ask exactly what the quoted package contains

Read the quote line by line. Look for the frame, covering, doors, vents, fasteners, anchors, base materials, controls, delivery, and assembly. If an item is shown in a picture but not listed, ask whether it is included. Keep the answer with the quote.

Ask for a separate list of exclusions. Common project gaps can include grading, foundation work, utilities, benches, crop supports, or disposal of packaging. The exact list depends on the supplier, so do not assume any one omission is universal. Your goal is to know the complete work required to create a usable greenhouse.

When comparing quotes, put matching items in matching rows. Mark an item as unknown if the description is unclear. A blank space should lead to a question, not an assumption that the item is free. Compare the final installed system and responsibilities, rather than choosing from totals built on different amounts of work.

Understand freight and delivery limits

A greenhouse may arrive as many long, heavy, or fragile pieces. Ask what kind of vehicle makes the delivery and what access it requires. Check driveway width, turning room, surface strength, overhead clearance, and the distance from the unloading point to the building site.

Find out who unloads the shipment and what equipment or people are required. Ask whether the delivery service places materials near the site or leaves them at another agreed point. These details can change labor needs and the timing of installation.

Plan a dry, safe storage area if assembly will not begin immediately. Follow the maker's instructions for storing panels, film, seals, and hardware. Keep parts labeled and protect them from accidental damage. Ask how to record missing or damaged items at delivery and what deadline applies to reporting them. Read the actual terms rather than relying on a general memory of another purchase.

Create a weather-aware installation schedule

Installation needs a suitable window, prepared ground, available labor, and all required parts. A delivery date is not necessarily the day the structure can be assembled. Ask the installer which conditions may delay work and what preparation must be complete before the crew arrives.

Build a small buffer into the plan. A delayed foundation, a missing part, or unsuitable wind can affect the schedule. Do not order valuable plants around an untested promise that the greenhouse will be ready on one exact day. Give those plants a safe alternative place if the project takes longer.

Write the order of work. Site preparation and any required foundation normally come before the frame; utilities and control work may have their own sequence. Use the supplier's approved process. A clear schedule reduces the chance that one contractor's unfinished task leaves another crew waiting or forces a part of the job to be done twice.

Know who is responsible for each decision

A project can involve the seller, freight company, installer, site contractor, electrician, and property owner. List each person's job and contact information. When a responsibility is shared or unclear, settle it before work starts. Small gaps can become large delays when everyone assumes someone else is handling them.

Ask who confirms the foundation dimensions, who checks the frame, and who sets the controls. Find out who is allowed to make changes to the supplied design. A casual adjustment may affect performance or warranty terms, so keep changes documented and approved by the appropriate person.

Keep one current project folder. It can be paper or digital, but it should contain the brief, drawings, specifications, quotes, agreements, approvals, and contact list. Record important answers in writing. This is not about making a simple purchase feel formal; it is about keeping several parts of one project connected.

Read warranty and service terms before paying

A warranty is a written promise with conditions. Read what is covered, how long the coverage lasts, and what action the owner must take. Different parts of a greenhouse may have different terms. Damage from weather, incorrect installation, or skipped maintenance may be treated differently under the actual agreement.

Ask how a claim begins. What information, photographs, proof of purchase, or inspection is required? Who pays shipping or labor when a covered part must be replaced? Do not assume the answer from the word warranty alone. Keep a copy of the terms that applied to your purchase.

Service also matters after the warranty period. Ask whether common replacement parts remain available and whether the company offers help interpreting a problem. A structure you can maintain with clear instructions and available parts may serve you better than one with impressive claims but unclear support.

Budget for the years after installation

Write two budgets: the money needed to open the greenhouse and the money needed to keep it useful. The opening budget includes all work and equipment before plants move in. The use budget includes growing supplies, energy if used, water, cleaning, repairs, and expected periodic replacements.

Use your own current quotes and realistic use plan. Do not turn a best-case crop value into guaranteed money. If the greenhouse is mainly for enjoyment, write that honestly. If the budget depends on sales, the crop and market plan deserve their own review before the purchase.

Create a reserve line for future work even when you do not know the exact year it will happen. Ask the supplier which parts usually require attention and follow the maintenance schedule. A budget that allows care and repair is more useful than spending every available dollar on the initial structure.

Write an acceptance checklist

Before the project is finished, walk through it with the installer or supplier's representative when available. Compare the installed work with the agreed scope. Look at doors, vents, fasteners, covering edges, drainage, paths, and control access. Use the maker's instructions for any checks that require technical judgment.

Ask for a demonstration of every control. Operate normal openings and shutdowns. Confirm how to read the sensors and what to do when a system fails. Keep manuals, settings, parts lists, and emergency contacts together. A greenhouse is not ready simply because the frame is standing.

Record any incomplete items and who will correct them. Follow the written agreement for handling those items and final payment. Do not climb onto the structure or test it in an unsafe way. The purpose of acceptance is to confirm useful, safe operation and clear responsibility, not to invent a stress test.

Open with a manageable first crop

Use the first growing cycle to learn the installed system. Begin with a crop group you can manage and enough empty space to reach equipment. Record high and low temperatures, water use, plant condition, and time spent. Compare the results with the plant goal in your original brief.

Check controls when outdoor conditions change. A setting that works during mild weather may need review as sun, wind, or cold changes. Follow crop guidance and the equipment instructions. Ask for help when the system does something you do not understand rather than making several large adjustments at once.

After the first month, write what is working, what needs correction, and what can wait. Keep that review separate from a wish list of new plants or accessories. The first success is a greenhouse you understand and can operate. A clear record helps you improve it without losing sight of the reason you bought it.

Try it in your own space

Make a one-page greenhouse project brief. Then build a quote comparison with rows for site work, base, frame, cover, ventilation, heat if needed, water, delivery, assembly, permits, and yearly care. Mark each row included, excluded, or unknown for two real proposals. Write the next question beside every unknown.

Your next-step checklist

  • Name crops, seasons, space, access, and essential controls in writing.
  • Confirm site conditions and local requirements before ordering.
  • Compare complete scope, exclusions, load assumptions, and replacement parts.
  • Plan delivery access, storage, installation sequence, and backup plant space.
  • Complete a safe operating demonstration and keep the project records.
