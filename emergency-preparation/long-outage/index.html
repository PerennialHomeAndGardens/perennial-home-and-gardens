Emergency Preparation

When several services go down.

No power, safe tap water, or refrigeration? Start with what keeps your household safe today. Then set up a way to replenish what you use.

Can we stay safely? I’m preparing ahead

Do these first

  1. Check people and the home.

    Check for injuries, immediate hazards, and anyone who needs powered medical equipment or temperature-sensitive medicine.

  2. Switch to safe water and keep the fridge closed.

    Use bottled water if the tap supply is unsafe or its status is unknown. Note when the power went out.

  3. Find out what’s open.

    Check official alerts for evacuation orders, drinking-water distribution, shelters, and charging or warming and cooling centers.

Can we stay safely?

Stay while you can meet the basics

The building is safe, temperatures are manageable, and you have safe water, workable sanitation, necessary medical support, and a way to get updates.

Move early when a basic need is at risk

Follow evacuation orders promptly. Plan to relocate before safe water, critical medication, medical-device power, or safe indoor temperatures run out. Use a safe route and an official shelter or other safe destination.

Find official alerts and shelters

For an outage that could last weeks or months, line up a reliable source of safe water, help from people nearby, and a place you can go. A pantry buys time. Water, sanitation, and medical needs determine whether staying remains practical.

Safe water

Start with a source you trust Use sealed bottled water, water from an official distribution point, or tap water your utility confirms is safe.

  • Read the advisory. A boil-water notice is different from a do-not-drink or do-not-use notice. Boiling, disinfecting and ordinary filters cannot make water contaminated with fuel or toxic chemicals safe. Use another source and follow local instructions.
  • For germs only. If authorities advise boiling, bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute, or three minutes above 6,500 feet. For cloudy water or when boiling is impossible, follow the CDC’s treatment steps and measurement chart. Follow the product directions for any treatment tablets.
  • Store and replenish. Start with at least one gallon per person per day for drinking and basic uses. Use clean food-grade containers with tight lids, label the fill date, and replace water you filled yourself every six months. Set aside extra for pets, hot weather, and special needs. Plan an official refill source and containers you can carry.

Food without refrigeration

Watch the clock Keep doors shut. An unopened refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours. An unopened freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours if full or 24 hours if half full.

  • Discard refrigerated perishables after four hours without power unless you kept them at 40°F or colder. Use a food thermometer and the federal chart. Never taste food to check whether it’s safe.
  • Start with meals that use little water and no fuel. Try ready-to-eat canned beans, fish or chicken paired with crackers, canned fruit or vegetables, or nut or seed butter. Use a manual can opener and open only what you’ll eat right away.
  • Build around shelf-stable foods you already eat. Rotate older items to the front. Keep dry staples cool and dry, and budget water and fuel for rice and dried beans. Keep any fuel-burning camp stove or grill outside.

Lighting and backup power

Start with what you have Gather headlamps, flashlights, battery lanterns, spare batteries, charged power banks, and a radio. Set a light and spare batteries where each person can find them in the dark.

  • Candles are optional. Choose battery lighting first. If you must use a candle, put it in a sturdy holder on a stable surface at least 12 inches from anything that can burn. Keep it away from children and pets. Put it out every time you leave the room or go to sleep. Keep flames away from oxygen equipment.
  • Generate a little power with portable solar. Use a commercially made solar panel with a compatible power station and the maker’s cables. Set up the panel in direct sun and follow the manual for weather protection and charging limits. Plug small devices into the station. A 5-watt lamp used for four hours needs about 20 watt-hours before losses. Test your actual equipment and allow for cloudy days. A charged USB power bank is a useful fallback for phones.
  • Plan bigger loads separately. Refrigerators, well pumps, and medical devices require more careful sizing and may have high start-up demand. Ask the device supplier about approved medical backup power. Have an electrician design any connection to household wiring. Never feed generator power into a wall outlet.
  • Keep fuel-burning generators outside. Use them more than 20 feet from doors, windows, and vents, with exhaust pointed away. Keep working battery-powered or battery-backed carbon monoxide alarms. Garages and porches are unsafe for generator use.

Sanitation and hygiene

Set up a simple handwashing station Use a clean covered jug with a spigot, safe water, soap, and a basin to catch runoff. Let the water flow over hands and keep the runoff away from clean supplies.

  • Check with the sewer utility or health department before using toilets if you suspect sewer or septic trouble. If the system is confirmed to be working and local guidance permits it, a tank toilet can often be flushed with non-drinking water.
  • If officials say to stop flushing, use an approved portable toilet or follow your local instructions for an emergency toilet and waste collection. Keep waste away from food, wells, and water sources.
  • Keep soap, trash bags, toilet paper, and cleaning supplies on hand. Use safe water for washing hands, especially after handling waste and before preparing food.

First aid and medicines

Keep care and prescriptions accessible Pack gloves, gauze, bandages, a thermometer, your medication list, and pharmacy contacts. Ask your pharmacist how to handle refills and medicines that require refrigeration.

  • Minor cuts and scrapes. Wash your hands, apply direct pressure with clean gauze or cloth to control bleeding, rinse the wound with clean running or bottled water, and cover it. For heavy bleeding, get emergency help immediately and keep steady pressure on the wound. Get prompt medical care for deep, dirty, or bite wounds, signs of infection, or questions about a tetanus booster.
  • Common cold supplies. Consider plain saline nasal spray, a thermometer, and a pain or fever medicine your pharmacist says is suitable for your household. Follow the label and check active ingredients. Many cold products contain acetaminophen, so avoid taking two acetaminophen-containing products together.
  • Personal needs come first. Have a relocation plan for anyone who depends on powered equipment, regular treatments, or medicine that cannot safely be kept at home during the outage. Ask the prescriber or pharmacist before using temperature-sensitive medicine that lost refrigeration.

Build a household reserve

Build the plan in stages. Test how you’ll obtain and carry supplies after stores or delivery services are disrupted.

Start with three days

Set aside a portable supply of water, ready-to-eat food, necessary medicine and supplies, light, and basic first aid. Choose meeting places and an out-of-area contact.

Build toward two weeks

Grow your home reserve a little at a time. Rotate pantry food, stage water and refill containers, test your lights and chargers, and write down local alert, shelter, pharmacy, and utility information.

Plan for weeks or months

Use a rotating pantry and a safe water resupply plan. Arrange check-ins and shared transport with people nearby. Estimate how long supplies last, when you’ll refill them, and what would make you relocate. A seasonal garden can supplement a pantry when conditions allow.

Try a short practice run. Keep essential equipment running and rehearse finding lights, making a no-cook meal, contacting your household, and collecting the items you’d take if you had to leave.
